In designSLAP Lab, Wake Forest University2026–

Anxiety's effect on triggered suppression: does anxiety disrupt suppression that has already been learned?

Attention is not governed by goals and salience alone: the observer's internal state also shapes which stimuli win. In the triggered suppression paradigm (Zhang & Gaspelin, 2026), participants learn that one color is usually irrelevant and stop looking at it. Anxiety is a good test case. It is well studied, can be induced within ethical limits, and is the state most likely to compete for the control resources that suppression depends on. This study asks whether an anxious state weakens that learned suppression.

Each participant completes 12 blocks of 40 visual search trials while an eye tracker records their gaze. In the neutral phase they hear harmless tones between trials. In the threat phase they are told an unpleasant sound may occur at any time, and unpredictable human screams (below 70 dB, about 6% of trials) replace the tones. Tones and screams follow the same schedule, so the only thing that changes is threat. The main measure is the share of first fixations that land on the color singleton; with 8 items in the array, chance is 12.5%.

Measuring involuntary startle reactions from webcam video with SLEAP

The eye tracker sees the eyes but not the shoulders, face or hands, which is where a startle shows up most. A webcam records the upper body during the task, and SLEAP tracks keypoints frame by frame. For each tone or scream I measure movement in the 1–2 seconds afterward, blinks, and time to return to baseline.

This gives an objective manipulation check that does not interrupt the task. It also shows whether reactions habituate across the threat phase, and whether trials with a strong reaction are followed by more distractor capture. The video measure is validated against pupil dilation and blinks from the eye tracker.

  1. RecordWebcam synced to trials by a screen flash
  2. TrackSLEAP keypoints on face and shoulders
  3. AlignPython: match keypoints to trial and sound times
  4. ModelR: mixed-effects models of reaction and capture

Progress: In October 2026 SLEAP was installed and tested on a local GPU, and a first single-instance keypoint model was trained on practice video of myself. Next is adding video from more volunteers so the model generalizes across faces and body shapes, then building the full pipeline, including timing sync, on pilot data.

  4. Epigenetic regulation of retinal ganglion cell degeneration and regeneration: Mechanisms and therapeutic insights

    Zile Zhao, Ke Li, Lingxuan He, Ming Yuan, Ajay Ashok, Dongfeng Chen*

    Review article · Schepens Eye Research Institute, Harvard Medical School

    In preparationFirst author
    Abstract

    Retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) are the output neurons of the retina and carry visual signals to the brain. Their degeneration is a key pathological feature of glaucoma and optic neuropathies. Emerging evidence suggests that epigenetic regulation, including DNA methylation and demethylation and histone acetylation and deacetylation, plays a pivotal role in both RGC degeneration and regeneration. We first examine how epigenetic modifications contribute to RGC degeneration through three pathological processes: apoptosis, inflammation and oxidative stress. DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs), TET dioxygenases, histone acetyltransferases (HATs) and histone deacetylases (HDACs) regulate these pathways by altering gene expression and chromatin accessibility. We then explore how epigenetic remodeling supports regeneration, highlighting the therapeutic potential of HDAC inhibitors and TET-mediated demethylation for axonal regrowth. Finally, we discuss how inflammation and oxidative stress influence epigenetic enzyme activity, disrupting the crosstalk between methylation and demethylation. Together, these findings show the dynamic roles of epigenetic mechanisms in governing RGC fate and suggest new targets for neuroprotective and regenerative therapies.

Epigenetic interactions in RGC fate: Linking degeneration and regeneration

Zile Zhao1,3, Lingxuan He1,3, Ke Li2, Ming Yuan1, Ajay Ashok3, Dongfeng Chen3*

1University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign · 2The Hong Kong Polytechnic University · 3Schepens Eye Research Institute, Mass Eye and Ear, Harvard Medical School

Review posterNot yet presentedFirst author
Abstract

Retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) are the output neurons of the retina and are essential for transmitting visual information to the brain. Their degeneration is a major pathological feature of glaucoma and optic neuropathies, while their limited regenerative capacity remains a central obstacle in optic nerve repair. This review poster summarizes how key epigenetic regulators, including DNMTs, TET enzymes, HATs and HDACs, contribute to RGC degeneration through apoptosis, neuroinflammation, oxidative stress and redox imbalance. It also highlights how epigenetic remodeling may support regeneration by altering chromatin accessibility and reactivating growth-associated gene programs. Finally, we discuss interactions among epigenetic pathways, emphasizing that RGC fate is shaped not by isolated enzymes but by dynamic crosstalk between methylation, demethylation and histone modification.
